Physics and Maths

This is not a revision resource like the other three. Both subjects ask for material years beyond where you are, and pretending otherwise would waste the time you have. What follows is where the reachable marks actually are, and what to leave alone.

Your category

Physics

The single most useful fact: you are given a formula sheet in the exam. Every equation below is printed on it, along with the constants. You do not need to memorise any of them.

What you are being tested on is recognising which formula the situation calls for. That is a skill you can practise in a few hours, and it is worth far more than trying to learn mechanics from a university textbook in eight weeks.

Here is the whole sheet, with what each equation is for. You don't have to learn them — you have to know which one a question is asking for, so read the middle column rather than the left one.

Motion

EquationWhat it saysExample
vf = vi + atSpeed after accelerating for a time. No distance involved.From rest at 3 m s⁻² for 4 s: v = 0 + 3×4 = 12 m s⁻¹
xf = xi + vit + ½at²How far you've gone after a time. Use when time is given.Dropped from rest for 3 s: x = 0 + 0 + ½×9.8×9 = 44 m

Three kinematics equations, and the trick is choosing between them: each one leaves out a different quantity. The first has no distance, the second no final speed, the third no time. Work out which quantity the question never mentions, and use the equation that omits it.

The energy equations are most often used in pairs. A ball falling converts Ug into KE, so mgh = ½mv² — and because m appears on both sides it cancels, which is why all objects fall at the same rate.

The last three are printed on the sheet even though optics doesn't appear in either topic list — one of several places where the syllabus documents don't quite agree with each other. If a formula is on the sheet, treat it as fair game.

The parallel axis line is worth noticing, because one of the syllabus's own sample questions asks for the moment of inertia of a rod about its end — and that is not printed on the sheet. You derive it from the two lines that are.

Constants given include G, c, the gas constant R, ε₀, Avogadro's number, the elementary charge, Planck's constant and the speed of sound in air. So anything asking you to recall a number is testing something other than memory.

Where the marks are

The exam starts easy and gets harder. The syllabus says so explicitly, and the sample questions confirm it — the opening questions are solvable in seconds by anyone who knows which formula to use. Your target is to clear those completely and not run out of time on them.

Worked example — a typical early question

Five resistors, each 30 Ω. What is the difference between their total resistance connected in series and connected in parallel?

Show answer

Series: just add them. 5 × 30 = 150 Ω.

Parallel: 1/R = 5 × (1/30) = 5/30 = 1/6, so R = 6 Ω.

Difference = 150 − 6 = 144 Ω.

Two formulas, both given to you, about twenty seconds of arithmetic. Questions like this are worth the same as the hard ones at the end. Clear them first.

Worked example — when the formula isn't enough

A planet keeps the same density but its radius doubles. What happens to the gravitational acceleration at its surface?

Show answer

The trap is to see g = GM/R² and say that doubling R quarters g. But M is not fixed — a bigger planet of the same density has more mass.

Mass = density × volume, and volume goes as R³. So M ∝ R³.

Therefore g ∝ R³/R² = R. Doubling the radius doubles g.

This is the shape of the harder questions: the formula is given, but you have to notice that one of its symbols is itself changing. Read what is held constant.

Years 10–11 — one warning

The formula sheet is not exhaustive. One of the syllabus's own sample questions asks for the work done by an ideal gas expanding at constant temperature, which needs W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) — a formula that is not on the sheet and involves a logarithm.

So treat the sheet as a floor, not a ceiling. If a question seems to need something that isn't printed, it probably does. It is also fine to skip that question.

How to practise

Past Physics Bowl papers, free at aapt.org, are the source the syllabus names. Do the first fifteen questions of two or three papers rather than one paper all the way through — the early questions are where your marks are, and that is what you should get fast at.

Maths

Be straight about this one. The syllabus lists Vieta's formulas, the binomial theorem, modular arithmetic, telescoping series, Heron's formula, stars and bars, and the pigeonhole principle. That is competition mathematics — a separate discipline with its own training tradition, not harder school maths.

It cannot be taught from scratch in eight weeks alongside four other subjects, and attempting it would cost you marks in biology and earth science that are genuinely within reach. So the plan for maths is deliberately small.

The difficulty benchmark is AMC10AMC8, a US contest. That matters, because those contests reward clever reasoning about elementary material far more than they reward advanced technique. A thoughtful student with no competition training can usually get the first several questions of an AMC paper right. That is your target.

Six things worth learning, in order

Each of these is quick, transfers immediately, and shows up constantly.

  • Complementary counting. When "at least one" appears, count the opposite and subtract from the total. Almost always faster.
  • The two special triangles. 45-45-90 has sides in ratio 1 : 1 : √2. 30-60-90 has 1 : √3 : 2. Recognising them removes most trigonometry.
  • Pythagorean triples. Memorise 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25 and their multiples. Spotting one saves a calculation and often reveals the intended route.
  • Casework, done neatly. Break a problem into cases that don't overlap and don't leave gaps, solve each, add. Unglamorous and very effective.
  • Vieta's formulas. For ax² + bx + c = 0, the roots sum to −b/a and multiply to c/a. Lets you answer questions about roots without finding them.
  • Last-digit and remainder arguments. To find the last digit of a large power, look at the cycle. 7¹ ends in 7, 7² in 9, 7³ in 3, 7⁴ in 1, then it repeats every four.
Worked example — complementary counting

How many three-digit numbers contain at least one digit 7?

Show answer

Counting directly means handling "exactly one 7", "exactly two", "exactly three" and avoiding double-counting. Slow and error-prone.

Instead count the ones with no 7 and subtract.

Total three-digit numbers: 100 to 999, so 900.

With no 7: the hundreds digit can be 1–9 except 7, so 8 choices. The tens and units can each be 0–9 except 7, so 9 choices each. That gives 8 × 9 × 9 = 648.

At least one 7 = 900 − 648 = 252.

Three lines instead of three cases. Whenever a question says "at least", check whether the complement is easier first.

In the exam

  • Read every maths question, but decide within about fifteen seconds whether you can see a route. If not, move on and come back.
  • Answers are multiple choice. If you can rule out options by size, sign or parity, do — an educated guess beats a blank.
  • Don't let maths eat time budgeted for biology or earth science. Those are where your preparation can actually show.

How to practise

Past AMC8AMC10 papers with full solutions are free on the Art of Problem Solving wiki. Do the first ten questions of three or four papers. Read the solutions to the ones you miss — not to memorise them, but to notice the moves that keep recurring, because a small number of ideas cover a lot of questions.

Half an hour a week is a sensible budget. More than that is coming out of subjects where it would do more good.

How to divide your time

Eight weeks, five subjects. If you split it evenly you will do badly at all five. A defensible split:

SubjectShare of your study timeBecause
Biology30%You've been taught most of it. Practice converts that into marks.
Earth science30%All new, but all learnable. The best return per hour on the paper.
Chemistry25%Calculations reward practice; the core is reachable.
Physics10%Formulas are given. Spend it on recognising which to use.
Maths5%Familiarity only. The rest cannot be built in the time.

Nobody is expected to score highly across all five. The syllabus says so, twice, in every subject. What separates students is not covering everything — it is being genuinely solid on the parts that were reachable.
